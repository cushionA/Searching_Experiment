Energies 2026 , 19 (20), 4893; https://doi.org/10.3390/en19204893 This article belongs to the Special Issue Smart Energy Management and Industrial Decarbonization: Artificial Intelligence, Digital Transformation, and Sustainable Energy Transition Version Notes Order Reprints Abstract Large language model (LLM) inference is an increasing component of AI energy demand, while the energy effects of weight quantization depend on workload phase and implementation. We benchmarked four LLM families—Gemma 4 E4B IT, Qwen3.5 4B, Nemotron 3 Nano 4B, and Ministral 3 3B Instruct—across Q4_K_M, Q6_K, Q8_0, and BF16 weight representations on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 using llama.cpp/CUDA. Five workloads separated prefill from autoregressive generation, and GPU energy was estimated from NVML telemetry. In PP-512, no statistically significant differences were detected under the employed measurement protocol; in PP-1024, precision affected total energy and mean GPU power. In all generation scenarios, model family, precision, and their interaction significantly affected the primary endpoints. Relative to BF16, Q4_K_M reduced dynamic energy per token by 63.6–85.7% and increased generation throughput by 85.2–160.2% across all four model families. Ministral 3 3B Instruct–Q4_K_M ranked first in TOPSIS, whereas Nemotron 3 Nano 4B–Q4_K_M required less GPU memory. Findings are limited to single-request execution on one RTX 4090/llama.cpp-CUDA stack; output quality was not assessed, and 50 ms NVML sampling limits interpretation of short prefill measurements. Keywords: large language models ; LLM inference ; weight quantization ; GPU energy consumption ; energy efficiency ; inference throughput ; TOPSIS ; Pareto optimization 1. Introduction The rapid development and deployment of large language models (LLMs) has increased attention to the computational and environmental costs of artificial intelligence. The concept of Green AI emphasizes that computational efficiency should be considered alongside predictive or generative performance when evaluating AI systems [ 1 ]. A systematic review of Green AI research similarly shows that energy monitoring, benchmarking, and deployment efficiency have become important research directions, although a substantial part of the earlier literature focused primarily on model training [ 2 ]. As generative models are deployed at scale, however, inference becomes a recurring operational workload and may therefore account for a substantial share of the energy associated with practical model use [ 3 , 4 ]. Consequently, evaluating the energy efficiency of inference is increasingly relevant not only from an environmental perspective but also for hardware utilization, operating costs, and deployment scalability. Energy efficiency cannot be characterized adequately by GPU power alone. Power represents the instantaneous rate of energy consumption, whereas the energy required for an inference request depends jointly on power and execution time. A configuration operating at lower average power may therefore consume more total energy if its execution time increases sufficiently. Likewise, request-level energy and token-normalized energy address different questions: the former reflects the cost of completing a request, while the latter facilitates comparison of processing efficiency. Recent benchmarking work has consequently emphasized the importance of measuring both power and energy and of aligning measurements with individual phases of LLM inference [ 5 ]. This distinction is particularly important because LLM inference consists of computationally different stages. During prompt processing, commonly referred to as the prefill phase, the input sequence is processed in parallel to construct the internal state required for generation. Autoregressive decoding subsequently generates tokens sequentially. Previous system-level characterization has shown that prompt processing tends to be relatively compute-intensive, whereas token generation is more strongly constrained by memory movement and exhibits different latency, utilization, and power characteristics [ 6 ]. The same optimization may therefore have substantially different effects in prefill and generation workloads. For this reason, energy measurements that aggregate the entire inference process without distinguishing workload composition can obscure phase-specific behavior. Quantization is one of the principal techniques used to reduce the computational and memory requirements of LLM inference. Post-training quantization methods such as GPTQ demonstrated that transformer weights can be represented at substantially reduced precision while maintaining useful model accuracy [ 7 ]. LLM.int8() introduced mixed-precision mechanisms that preserve higher precision for selected outlier dimensions while performing most matrix operations at 8-bit precision [ 8 ]. SmoothQuant extended efficient post-training quantization to 8-bit weights and activations [ 9 ], while AWQ demonstrated hardware-oriented low-bit weight-only quantization capable of reducing memory requirements and accelerating token generation [ 10 ]. More recent work has continued to investigate fine-grained and mixed low-precision representations explicitly from the perspective of energy-efficient LLM inference [ 11 ]. The representation formats evaluated in this study should not be interpreted as uniform model-wide bit widths. Within the GGUF/llama.cpp ecosystem, Q8_0 uses block-wise quantization with 8-bit weight values and block-level scaling, whereas Q6_K and Q4_K belong to the K-quant family and use larger super-block structures together with quantized weights and associated scaling metadata. Q4_K_M is a model-level “mostly Q4_K” representation and may retain selected tensors in higher-precision quantized formats. Consequently, Q4_K_M, Q6_K, and Q8_0 are treated here as categorical weight-representation schemes rather than as exact 4-, 6-, and 8-bit representations of every model tensor. BF16 serves as the higher-precision reference format. Recent research has increasingly shifted from estimating the general computational footprint of AI toward direct measurement and characterization of inference energy. It was shown that AI energy consumption must be interpreted in relation to workload characteristics, hardware platform, and accelerator utilization. Kaur et al. examined the energy and emissions associated with LLM inference across application domains, highlighting the importance of workload-specific measurement [ 12 ]. At the accelerator level, Kang and Park demonstrated that measured power and energy efficiency depend on hardware characteristics and workload execution on heterogeneous AI accelerators [ 13 ], while earlier work by Kang et al. showed that GPU-based inference efficiency is closely linked to resource allocation and performance requirements [ 14 ]. Together, these studies reinforce the need to evaluate operational energy empirically rather than infer it solely from model size or theoretical computational complexity. Recent LLM-specific benchmarks further demonstrate that inference energy is highly sensitive to deployment conditions. Fernandez et al. found that the energy benefits of common inference optimizations vary substantially with workload geometry, software framework, GPU architecture, decoding strategy, and serving configuration [ 15 ]. Similarly, the ML.ENERGY Benchmark introduced systematic inference-energy measurements across multiple generative-AI models and tasks and demonstrated that energy consumption can vary considerably across otherwise functionally equivalent deployment configurations [ 16 ]. Oviedo et al. further showed that per-query energy depends strongly on output length, model and serving configuration, and hardware assumptions, particularly for reasoning-intensive and test-time-scaling workloads [ 17 ]. These findings indicate that energy efficiency is a system-level property emerging from the interaction among model characteristics, workload structure, software implementation, and hardware platform rather than from any single parameter in isolation. Hardware class represents an additional source of variation in inference-energy measurements. A high-end consumer GPU such as the RTX 4090 operates under different memory-capacity, memory-bandwidth, low-precision execution, and power-management constraints than server-oriented data-center accelerators. Data-center GPUs are designed for sustained accelerator workloads and typically provide substantially different memory systems and deployment characteristics. Cross-platform LLM inference studies have demonstrated that such hardware differences can materially affect both throughput and energy consumption [ 15 , 16 ]. Accordingly, the present RTX 4090 results should be interpreted as a controlled consumer-GPU benchmark rather than as a quantitative proxy for data-center deployments. Existing quantization studies have predominantly focused on model size, memory footprint, accuracy retention, and inference speed [ 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 ], whereas the emerging literature on LLM energy has primarily examined differences among workloads, model architectures, serving configurations, and hardware platforms [ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 ]. Comparatively less attention has been devoted to isolating the effect of weight representation format on directly measured GPU energy while holding the hardware platform and inference backend constant. Limited controlled evidence is available on whether reductions in weight representation precision translate consistently into lower request- and token-level energy across different LLM families and across computationally distinct inference phases. This distinction is important because the energy benefit of quantization may depend not only on the amount of model data transferred but also on model-specific execution characteristics, dequantization overhead, GPU kernel efficiency, and the relative contribution of prompt processing and autoregressive decoding. The present study addresses this gap by evaluating four weight representation formats—Q4_K_M, Q6_K, Q8_0, and BF16—across four LLM families under a controlled single-GPU environment. Prefill-only and autoregressive generation workloads are analyzed separately, while energy measurements are considered together with throughput, GPU memory requirements, and mean GPU power. By holding the GPU platform and inference backend constant, the design enables within-family assessment of weight-representation effects with fewer hardware- and runtime-related confounders. Against this background, the study addresses the following research questions: RQ1. How does weight representation format affect GPU energy consumption and inference performance within the tested LLM families? RQ2. Are the effects of weight representation consistent between prefill-only and generation workloads, or do they depend on model family and workload phase? RQ3. Which model–precision configurations provide the most favorable joint trade-off among dynamic energy per token, generation throughput, GPU memory use, and mean GPU power? The contribution of the study is threefold. First, it provides a controlled comparison of 16 model–precision configurations executed on the same GPU and inference backend, enabling within-family evaluation of weight representation effects under a common experimental environment. Second, it explicitly separates prefill-only workloads from autoregressive generation and evaluates energy at both request and token levels, thereby accounting for the different computational characteristics of these inference phases. Third, it combines statistical inference with multi-criteria assessment using TOPSIS and Pareto dominance, allowing energy consumption, inference throughput, GPU memory requirements, and power demand to be considered jointly rather than in isolation. By integrating phase-specific energy measurements, within-family quantization comparisons, and multi-criteria evaluation within a single controlled experimental framework, the study complements existing research on LLM energy consumption and quantization with empirical evidence of the associated efficiency trade-offs. The novelty of the study therefore lies not in proposing a new quantization algorithm, but in isolating representation-format effects through a phase-aware, within-family GPU-energy benchmark under a fixed hardware and runtime environment, supported by factorial inference and complementary multi-criteria analysis. Importantly, all reported percentage improvements are specific to the NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU running llama.cpp with the CUDA backend and should not be interpreted as generalizable performance gains across other hardware architectures or inference frameworks. 2. Materials and Methods 2.1. Experimental Design and Benchmark Environment The study was conducted as a controlled benchmark comparing the GPU energy consumption and inference performance of four large language model families: Gemma 4 E4B IT, Qwen3.5 4B, Nemotron 3 Nano 4B, and Ministral 3 3B Instruct. Each model family was evaluated using four weight representation formats: Q4_K_M, Q6_K, Q8_0, and BF16, resulting in a total of 16 model–precision configurations. Throughout the study, Q4_K_M, Q6_K, Q8_0, and BF16 were treated as categorical weight representation formats rather than as uniform bit-width representations of every model tensor. Accordingly, the term precision format used in the statistical analyses refers to the evaluated weight representation format. All experiments were performed on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 graphics processing unit (GPU) with a power limit of 450 W. The system used NVIDIA driver version 591.74 and a Windows x86_64 operating environment. Inference was performed using llama.cpp with the CUDA backend. All measurements were conducted on the same hardware and software platform. The experiments were performed using llama.cpp build 9950 (commit 961e4b26a) with CUDA 12.4. All model layers were offloaded to the GPU. The context size was set to 4096 tokens, with a batch size of 512 and a micro-batch size of 256. The key and value tensors of the KV cache were stored in FP16 format, and Flash Attention was enabled. These inference settings were kept constant across all model–precision configurations and benchmark scenarios. llama.cpp was selected because it provides native support for the evaluated GGUF weight-representation formats and CUDA GPU offloading within a single inference runtime. This allowed all model–format configurations to be evaluated using the same software backend, thereby avoiding framework changes as an additional experimental factor. 2.2. Benchmark Scenarios Five benchmark scenarios were designed to distinguish prompt processing from autoregressive token generation: PP-512—processing a 512-token prompt without the actual token generation phase; PP-1024—processing a 1024-token prompt without the actual token generation phase; TG-256—generating 256 tokens from a minimal single-token input; PG-512/256—processing a 512-token prompt and generating 256 output tokens; PG-2048/256—processing a 2048-token prompt and generating 256 output tokens. One inference request was executed in each iteration. No continuous batching, concurrent requests, or request scheduling was used. This design was intended to provide a controlled baseline for attributing observed differences to the model–representation configuration rather than to serving-policy effects; it was not intended to reproduce a maximum-throughput production serving environment. Each model–precision–scenario configuration was evaluated in 10 independent repetitions, resulting in 10 observations per configuration within each benchmark scenario. The actual numbers of input and output tokens matched the target values specified for the corresponding scenario. The scenarios were analyzed separately because prompt processing and autoregressive token generation represent computationally different inference workloads and because the duration of the shortest prefill-only requests approached the temporal resolution of the GPU power telemetry. 2.3. GPU Power and Energy Measurement GPU telemetry was recorded using the NVIDIA Management Library (NVML) with a nominal sampling interval of 50 ms. The same 50 ms polling interval was retained across all runs to maintain a consistent measurement protocol. Because the power telemetry exposed through NVML is itself updated at hardware- and driver-dependent intervals, increasing the software polling frequency does not necessarily yield proportionally finer or independent power measurements. The resulting telemetry was therefore not treated as high-resolution power tracing, particularly for short prefill-only requests. Immediately before each inference request, GPU idle power was determined while the corresponding model remained loaded. Total GPU energy for an inference request was calculated by integrating GPU power over the request duration (1): E total = ∫ t 0 t 1 P t dt (1) where E total —total GPU energy consumed during the request [J]; P ( t )—instantaneous GPU power [W]; t _0—request start time; t _1—request end time; Dynamic energy was defined as the energy exceeding the cost corresponding to the measured idle power according to Equation (2). E dyn = E total − P idle ∙ t (2) where E total —total GPU energy consumed during the request [J]; P idle —GPU idle power measured immediately before the request [W]; t —request duration [s]. For the generation scenarios (TG-256, PG-512/256, and PG-2048/256), the principal energy and performance metrics were: Dynamic GPU energy for the complete request [J]; Dynamic GPU energy per processed token [J/token]; Dynamic GPU energy per generated output token [J/output token]; Generation throughput [tokens/s]. Maximum GPU memory utilization and mean GPU power were additionally retained for the multi-criteria hardware-efficiency analysis. For the prefill-only scenarios (PP-512 and PP-1024), the principal interpretable metrics were total request energy [J], prompt-processing throughput [tokens/s], and execution time [s]. Dynamic energy was not interpreted in the prefill-only scenarios because request durations were close to the telemetry sampling resolution. Consequently, subtraction of the idle-energy baseline frequently produced zero values. Similarly, the technical value of generation throughput recorded for requests without actual output-token generation was not interpreted. This distinction was maintained throughout the analysis to avoid attributing greater measurement precision to short prefill-only workloads than was supported by the temporal resolution of the telemetry. 2.4. Statistical Analysis Descriptive statistics included the arithmetic mean, standard deviation, coefficient of variation, median, interquartile range, and range. For the mean and median, 95% confidence intervals were estimated using the percentile bootstrap method with 5000 replications and a fixed seed for the pseudorandom number generator. Each benchmark scenario was analyzed separately. The effects of model family, precision format, and their interaction were assessed using a two-factor linear model (3): Y { ijk } = μ + α i + β j + ( αβ ) { ij } + ε { ijk } (3) where Y ijk —value of the analyzed dependent variable; μ —overall mean; α i —effect of the i -th model family; β j —effect of the j -th precision format; ( αβ ) ij —model family × precision format interaction; ε ijk —residual error. The factors were coded using sum-to-zero contrasts, and their effects were assessed using Type III analysis of variance. Positive energy and time variables, as well as the corresponding positive-valued performance variables included in the statistical models, were transformed using the natural logarithm. Because heteroscedasticity was present, standard errors and test statistics were calculated using the heteroscedasticity-consistent HC3 covariance estimator. Effect sizes for the factorial analyses were reported as partial eta squared η p 2 . For the predefined primary endpoints, the family-wise probability of a Type I error was controlled using the Holm procedure. For exploratory results of the factorial models, the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate. All statistical tests were two-sided. Planned pairwise comparisons included comparisons between precision formats within each model family and comparisons between model families within a given precision format. Parametric comparisons were performed using Welch’s t -test. The reported measures included differences in means with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, ratios of means, and Hedges’ effect sizes. As a sensitivity analysis, corresponding comparisons were additionally evaluated using the Mann–Whitney test and Cliff’s delta. For the planned pairwise analyses, p -values were adjusted using the Holm procedure separately within scientifically interpretable contrast families defined by benchmark scenario, endpoint, comparison type, and model or precision stratum. 2.5. Multi-Criteria Hardware-Efficiency Analysis For the three generation scenarios, hardware efficiency was additionally evaluated using the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS). Four criteria were included: Dynamic energy per processed token—lower values were preferred; Generation throughput—higher values were preferred; Maximum GPU memory utilization—lower values were preferred; Mean GPU power—lower values were preferred. The respective TOPSIS weights were 0.55 for dynamic energy per processed token, 0.25 for generation throughput, 0.10 for maximum GPU memory utilization, and 0.10 for mean GPU power. The weights were defined a priori to represent the energy-oriented evaluation objective of the study; they were not estimated from the observed performance of the evaluated configurations and should not be interpreted as universally optimal TOPSIS weights. The weighting scheme was designed to reflect the energy-oriented hardware-efficiency perspective of the study. Dynamic energy per processed token was assigned the highest weight (0.55) because it directly quantifies the energy required to process a unit of model output and therefore represents the primary efficiency outcome considered in this work. Generation throughput was assigned the second-highest weight (0.25), ensuring that energy efficiency was evaluated jointly with inference performance. Together, these two criteria accounted for 80% of the TOPSIS weighting and represented the primary operational-efficiency dimension. Maximum GPU memory utilization and mean GPU power were assigned lower and equal weights (0.10 each), as they were considered complementary hardware-resource criteria rather than primary measures of inference efficiency. In particular, mean GPU power is not independent of energy consumption, since energy corresponds to power integrated over execution time. Assigning a comparatively low weight to mean power therefore also reduces the risk of disproportionately emphasizing related energy characteristics in the composite score. To complement the weight-dependent TOPSIS ranking, Pareto non-dominated configurations were identified independently of the TOPSIS scores. A configuration was considered Pareto non-dominated when no alternative configuration performed at least as well on all included criteria while performing strictly better on at least one criterion. TOPSIS rankings were interpreted exclusively as comparisons of hardware-resource efficiency under the adopted criteria and weighting scheme. Because the study did not independently evaluate response quality, task accuracy, factuality, reasoning ability, or other measures of model utility, the resulting rankings were not interpreted as rankings of the overall quality of the language models. 3. Results In PP-512, under the employed measurement protocol, the factorial analysis did not detect statistically significant effects of model family, precision format, or their interaction on total request energy, prompt-processing throughput, or execution time after false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Given the short request duration relative to the 50 ms telemetry interval, however, the PP-512 energy comparison is considered measurement-constrained and inconclusive with respect to small between-configuration differences. The absence of statistical significance should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of equivalent energy consumption among the evaluated configurations. The precision effect for total energy was close to the adopted significance threshold ( F = 3.16 , p FDR = 0.054 , η p 2 = 0.121 ). The Gemma 4 E4B IT–Q4_K_M configuration achieved the lowest average total energy in PP-512. The next-best result was achieved by the Ministral 3 3B Instruct–Q4_K_M. Given the absence of statistically significant effects in the factorial model and the overlapping confidence intervals, these numerical differences were not interpreted as evidence of superiority of one configuration over another. In PP-1024, an effect of precision on total Energy ( F = 5.96, p FDR = 0.0024, η p 2 = 0.203 ) and on average GPU power ( F = 5.01, p FDR = 0.0063, η p 2 = 0.1790 ) was observed. No significant model family effects or interactions were found for these variables. No significant effects were found for prompt processing throughput or execution time either. In terms of total energy, the Ministral 3 3B Instruct–Q4_K_M achieved the lowest value in PP-1024. This configuration also achieved the highest average prompt throughput, at 3493.7 tokens/s, 95% CI [2804.6; 4182.9]. Differences between quantization variants were assessed using Welch’s t -test and the Mann–Whitney U test, with Holm correction applied for multiple comparisons; effect sizes were additionally quantified using Cliff’s Δ. Across all evaluated models in the PG-512/256 and PG-2048/256 scenarios, Q4_K_M consistently exhibited the lowest mean dynamic energy consumption per request. Relative to Q6_K, Q4_K_M reduced mean energy consumption by approximately 26–54%, depending on the model and workload, while substantially larger reductions were observed relative to BF16. For example, for Ministral 3 3B Instruct in PG-512/256, mean dynamic energy consumption was 47.3 J for Q4_K_M, compared with 102.1 J for Q6_K, 117.4 J for Q8_0, and 309.2 J for BF16. However, Nemotron was the more favorable configuration in conditions where available GPU memory is the primary constraint. Discussion 4.1. Principal Findings The results demonstrate that the relationship between weight precision and GPU energy efficiency is strongly dependent on workload phase and model architecture. The clearest pattern was observed during autoregressive generation. Across all three generation scenarios, model family, precision format, and their interaction significantly affected the primary endpoints, with very large effect sizes for both model family and precision. The significant interaction is particularly important because it indicates that the benefit associated with a given weight representation cannot be interpreted independently of the model on which it is applied. Within all four tested model families, Q4_K_M was substantially more energy-efficient and faster than BF16 during generation. Depending on the model and scenario, Q4_K_M reduced mean dynamic energy per processed token by 63.6–85.7% and increased generation throughput by 85.2–160.2%. These improvements were statistically robust across the planned Q4_K_M–BF16 comparisons. The magnitude of the effect therefore indicates that, in the tested environment, aggressive weight quantization was not merely a memory-saving mechanism; it translated into substantial reductions in GPU energy required for token generation. The results were less uniform in the prefill-only workloads. No statistically supported effects of model family, precision, or their interaction were detected for total request energy, prompt throughput, or execution time in PP-512. In PP-1024, precision affected total energy and average GPU power, but corresponding effects on prompt throughput and execution time were not significant. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence that the configurations are equivalent during prefill. Rather, they indicate that statistically supported differences were limited under the adopted workload and measurement design. Consequently, the answer to the question posed in the article title is conditional. Lower precision produced very large and consistent benefits when Q4_K_M was compared with BF16 during the tested generation workloads, but the same strength of evidence was not observed for short prefill-only requests. Nominal precision therefore cannot be treated as a universal predictor of request-level GPU energy across all inference phases. 4.2. Why the Effect of Quantization Depends on the Workload Phase The difference between prefill and generation is consistent with established characterizations of LLM inference. Prompt processing and autoregressive decoding have different computational profiles: prefill is relatively compute-intensive, whereas token generation is more strongly influenced by repeated weight access and memory traffic [ 6 ]. Weight quantization directly reduces the amount of model data that must be stored and transferred and can therefore be particularly advantageous in workloads where memory movement limits performance. AWQ similarly identifies memory size and memory bandwidth as key constraints in LLM serving and demonstrates substantial generation speedups from hardware-oriented weight-only quantization [ 10 ]. This provides a plausible mechanism for the strong Q4_K_M advantage observed during generation. A smaller weight representation may reduce memory traffic sufficiently to increase token throughput, shorten execution time, and thereby lower energy per token even if the instantaneous GPU power does not decrease proportionally. Importantly, the present experiment did not directly decompose energy into arithmetic, memory-transfer, cache, or dequantization components. The mechanism should therefore be interpreted as being consistent with previous system-level findings rather than as a directly measured causal decomposition. The prefill results require additional caution because the requests were short relative to the nominal 50 ms NVML sampling interval. The study appropriately refrained from interpreting dynamically baseline-corrected energy for these cases. This measurement constraint reduces the ability to distinguish small differences between configurations during short prompt-processing workloads. Therefore, the absence of statistically significant differences in PP-512 should not be interpreted as proof that quantization has no influence on prefill energy. The results also demonstrate why power and energy should not be used interchangeably. A configuration may have higher or similar mean power while still consuming less energy if it finishes the request substantially faster. Conversely, reducing mean GPU power alone does not guarantee improved energy efficiency. For deployment decisions, energy per completed request or per token should therefore be considered together with throughput and latency. 4.3. Model Family and Quantization Format Interact The very large main effects of precision were accompanied by substantial model × precision interactions. This result reinforces the conclusion that quantization should be evaluated as a property of a complete model–runtime–hardware configuration rather than as an isolated numerical parameter. GPTQ, LLM.int8(), SmoothQuant, and AWQ already demonstrate that quantization performance depends strongly on how weights, activations, scaling factors, and outliers are represented and processed [ 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 ]. Recent low-precision research similarly emphasizes that the detailed numerical representation and hardware execution strategy can matter as much as nominal bit width itself [ 11 ]. The present results extend this observation to GPU energy consumption. Although Q4_K_M outperformed BF16 within every tested model family during generation, the magnitude of the reduction differed substantially among models. For Ministral 3 3B Instruct, Q4_K_M reduced dynamic energy per token by 84.0–85.7% relative to BF16 while increasing throughput by 105.2–160.2%. This was one of the strongest effects observed in the benchmark. The cross-model comparison between the two strongest Q4_K_M configurations further illustrates the architectural component. Ministral–Q4_K_M used 37.1–42.6% less dynamic energy per token than Nemotron–Q4_K_M across the three generation scenarios and achieved approximately 10.9–12.1% higher mean throughput. At the same time, Nemotron required substantially less GPU memory. Thus, even after the same nominal quantization format was selected, the two model families occupied different positions in the energy–performance–memory design space. Cross-model comparisons must nevertheless be interpreted as comparisons of complete systems. The models differ in architecture, parameterization, tokenizer behavior, and potentially other implementation characteristics. They should therefore not be interpreted as a controlled estimate of the causal effect of model architecture alone. The strongest evidence regarding precision comes from within-family comparisons, where the underlying model is held constant. 4.4. Multi-Criteria Efficiency: TOPSIS and Pareto Analysis Considering a single metric would obscure several practically important trade-offs. Ministral 3 3B Instruct–Q4_K_M achieved the lowest dynamic energy per token and the highest generation throughput among the leading configurations and consequently ranked first in TOPSIS in all three generation scenarios, with scores of 0.981–0.982. Nemotron 3 Nano 4B–Q4_K_M ranked second in each scenario, with scores of 0.917–0.932. These two configurations were also the only solutions located on the Pareto frontier in all three generation scenarios. The Pareto result is particularly useful because it does not require assigning explicit weights to optimization criteria. Neither configuration completely dominated the other: Ministral offered superior energy efficiency and throughput, whereas Nemotron required approximately 4.9 GiB of GPU memory compared with approximately 6.4 GiB for Ministral. The appropriate configuration therefore depends on the deployment constraint. Under the adopted TOPSIS weights, dynamic energy per token and throughput accounted jointly for 80% of the decision weight. Consequently, the lower energy requirement and higher throughput of Ministral outweighed its approximately 30% larger GPU-memory footprint. If memory capacity were the dominant operational constraint, however, Nemotron would represent the more favorable option. This finding illustrates an important deployment principle: the most energy-efficient configuration is not necessarily the optimal configuration for every system. GPU memory may determine whether several model instances can be hosted simultaneously, while throughput may determine service capacity and energy may dominate operating costs. Multi-criteria analysis therefore complements single-metric benchmarking, but its results must always be interpreted in relation to the selected criteria and weights. 4.5. Relation to Previous Research The substantial performance improvements associated with low-precision weights are consistent with earlier quantization research. GPTQ showed that 3- and 4-bit post-training weight quantization can substantially reduce LLM storage and enable faster inference while retaining useful accuracy [ 7 ]. LLM.int8() demonstrated the value of mixed-precision treatment of outliers [ 8 ], while SmoothQuant and AWQ reported substantial memory and performance benefits from hardware-oriented quantization strategies [ 9 , 10 ]. BlockDialect further demonstrates continuing interest in explicitly energy-efficient low-precision inference [ 11 ]. The present study differs from much of this literature by focusing directly on measured GPU energy rather than treating memory reduction or inference speed as proxies for energy efficiency. This distinction is important because faster inference does not mathematically guarantee lower energy consumption unless the accompanying power profile is also considered. The present findings are also consistent with recent empirical studies showing that LLM inference energy is strongly dependent on workload and deployment conditions. Fernandez et al. demonstrated that the energy impact of inference optimizations varies substantially across workload geometries, software stacks, and GPU platforms [ 15 ], while the ML.ENERGY Benchmark showed large differences in inference energy among model, task, and deployment configurations [ 16 ]. Similarly, Oviedo et al. reported a strong dependence of per-query energy on token generation requirements and serving assumptions [ 17 ]. The present results extend this body of evidence by demonstrating that, even when the GPU platform and inference backend are held constant, weight representation format interacts strongly with model family and workload phase. The findings also complement recent work published in Energies . Kaur et al. emphasized the importance of workload-specific assessment when estimating the operational energy and emissions associated with LLM inference [ 12 ], whereas Kang and Park demonstrated that accelerator-level energy efficiency depends on the characteristics of the execution platform [ 13 ]. The present study contributes a more narrowly controlled perspective by examining the energy–performance consequences of weight representation under a fixed single-GPU environment. This design does not replace broader system-level benchmarking, but allows the contribution of quantization format to be evaluated with fewer hardware-related confounders. The findings also align with the broader Green AI principle that computational efficiency should be measured explicitly rather than inferred from model size or theoretical operation counts [ 1 , 2 ]. The marked differences observed between prefill and generation are consistent with recent calls for phase-aware inference benchmarking and for the joint reporting of request-level and token-normalized energy metrics [ 5 , 15 , 16 , 17 ]. At the same time, the present results do not establish the environmental footprint of the tested models in a full life-cycle sense. The measurements concern operational GPU energy only. They do not include embodied hardware impacts, data-center cooling, power-delivery overhead, CPU and system energy, or the carbon intensity of the electricity supply. Consequently, the results should be described as GPU energy-efficiency findings rather than direct estimates of carbon emissions or comprehensive environmental sustainability. 4.6. Interpretation of Token-Normalized Energy An additional methodological point concerns comparisons of dynamic energy per processed token between workload scenarios. In TG-256, nearly all processed tokens belong to the autoregressive generation phase, whereas the PG-512/256 and PG-2048/256 scenarios contain progressively larger proportions of prompt tokens. Because prompt processing and token generation have different computational characteristics, the denominator in the J/token metric represents a different mixture of work in each scenario. Accordingly, the decrease in absolute J/token values observed when moving from TG-256 to workloads containing longer prompts should not be interpreted simply as evidence that longer prompts intrinsically improve energy efficiency. Token-normalized energy is most directly comparable between configurations evaluated within the same scenario. Request-level energy, energy per output token, throughput, and workload composition should be considered alongside J/token when comparisons are made across scenarios. This distinction is particularly relevant in LLM energy benchmarking because normalization can strongly affect conclusions. A metric that is appropriate for comparing two implementations under the same workload may become misleading when prompt length, generated-token count, or batching structure changes substantially. 4.7. Practical Implications Several practical implications follow from the results. First, weight quantization should be benchmarked on the intended model–GPU–runtime combination rather than selected solely based on nominal precision. The substantial model × precision interaction indicates that the magnitude of the energy benefit is architecture-dependent. Second, optimization should be workload-aware. Systems dominated by autoregressive generation may obtain very large benefits from low-bit weight representations, whereas the gains for short prefill workloads may be smaller or more difficult to measure reliably. Applications with different prompt-to-output ratios may therefore favor different deployment configurations. Third, throughput and energy should be evaluated jointly. In the present benchmark, the most energy-efficient generation configurations were also among the fastest, indicating that higher throughput and lower energy per token need not represent conflicting objectives. Nevertheless, GPU memory introduces an additional constraint that can change the preferred deployment choice. Finally, energy benchmarking should report sufficient context to make results reproducible: the specific model artifact, quantization format, inference backend, hardware platform, software version, workload composition, energy-measurement method, and normalization denominator are all relevant to interpretation. Energy values should not be generalized across hardware or inference engines without validation. 4.8. Limitations and Future Work The study has several limitations. First, all experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090. The results therefore characterize the tested consumer-GPU environment and should not be generalized directly to other NVIDIA architectures, data-center accelerators, or non-NVIDIA hardware. Different GPUs provide different low-precision arithmetic capabilities, memory bandwidth, cache hierarchies, and power-management behavior. Second, inference was performed using llama.cpp with a CUDA backend in a Windows environment. Quantization performance is sensitive to kernel implementation and runtime optimization; consequently, the numerical results may differ under alternative frameworks such as vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, or other CUDA/Linux serving stacks. Third, the energy measurements represent GPU energy derived from NVML telemetry rather than whole-system electricity consumption. CPU activity, host memory, storage, power-supply losses, and cooling overhead were outside the measurement boundary. The results should therefore be interpreted specifically as GPU-level operational energy. Fourth, the 50 ms nominal telemetry interval limits the temporal resolution available for very short requests. This is particularly relevant to the prefill-only scenarios and is one reason why dynamic baseline-corrected energy was not interpreted for those workloads. Fifth, one request was executed per iteration. The benchmark therefore does not reproduce production environments involving continuous batching, concurrent users, request scheduling, or high GPU utilization. Such operating modes may alter both throughput and energy efficiency. Sixth, the evaluated prompts extended to 2048 input tokens. Long-context inference may exhibit different relationships among attention computation, KV-cache activity, memory traffic, and quantization. Future work should therefore include longer contexts and varying input/output ratios. Seventh, response quality was not measured. The TOPSIS and Pareto analyses consequently evaluate hardware-resource efficiency rather than overall model utility. A configuration that minimizes energy per token may be less attractive if quantization materially reduces task accuracy, reasoning performance, factuality, or other quality dimensions. Future work should combine energy measurements with within-model quality-retention metrics and construct quality–energy Pareto frontiers. Eighth, TOPSIS rankings depend on the selected weights. Although Pareto analysis provides a weight-independent complement, future work should include a sensitivity analysis to determine whether rankings remain stable under alternative priorities for energy, throughput, memory, and power. Although the evaluated formats can be ordered approximately by nominal precision, Q4_K_M, Q6_K, and Q8_0 are distinct block-wise representation schemes rather than points on a uniform continuous bit-width scale. For this reason, the revised analysis evaluates the full set of formats through within-model pairwise comparisons instead of assuming a linear relationship between nominal bit width and energy consumption. Future work should extend this analysis across additional quantization schemes, hardware architectures, and inference backends. 5. Conclusions This study examined whether lower weight precision necessarily translates into lower GPU energy consumption during LLM inference. In the tested NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 and llama.cpp/CUDA environment, the strongest conclusion concerns autoregressive generation: Q4_K_M substantially outperformed BF16 within all four evaluated model families, reducing dynamic energy per token by 63.6–85.7% while simultaneously increasing generation throughput by 85.2–160.2%. These effects were statistically robust and demonstrate that aggressive weight quantization can provide major energy–performance benefits during generation. However, the results do not justify treating nominal precision as a universal or architecture-independent predictor of energy use. The significant model × precision interactions show that the magnitude of the benefit depends on the model family, while the substantially weaker findings in the short prefill-only scenarios demonstrate that the effect is also workload-phase-dependent. Among the evaluated generation configurations, Ministral 3 3B Instruct–Q4_K_M provided the most favorable combination of energy efficiency and throughput and ranked first in TOPSIS across all three scenarios. Nemotron 3 Nano 4B–Q4_K_M consistently ranked second and offered a substantially smaller GPU-memory footprint. Both configurations belonged to the Pareto frontier, illustrating that the preferred deployment option depends on the relative importance of energy, throughput, memory capacity, and power. Overall, the results indicate that quantization formats should be evaluated empirically on the intended model–hardware–runtime combination. Deployment decisions should use request-level and token-level energy together with throughput, latency, power, and memory requirements rather than relying on nominal bit width alone. Because model quality was not measured, the identified hardware-efficiency rankings should not be interpreted as rankings of overall model quality or usability. The numerical effect sizes reported here should therefore be interpreted as specific to the tested RTX 4090–llama.cpp/CUDA environment and should be independently validated before being extrapolated to data-center accelerators, alternative GPU architectures, or production serving frameworks.
