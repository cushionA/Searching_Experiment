Abstract
Hierarchical language generation separates continuous semantic modeling from discrete text
realization, but its effectiveness depends on what information the latent space should compress
and how the discrete generator should use it. We propose Cola2, which jointly studies continuous
semantic compression, the discrete reconstruction–generation balance, and their co-adaptation.
Cola2 dynamically segments text into semantic spans, controls the compression rate with an
auxiliary-loss-free negative-feedback mechanism, and combines reconstruction and generation
supervision to learn compact and predictive latent representations. A block-causal masked
diffusion model (MDM) decoder is jointly trained under clean, noisy, and dropped latent conditions,
and classifier-free guidance (CFG) adjusts the balance between semantic reconstruction and
autonomous generation. During training, token-prediction gradients update the encoder through
the latents, driving the continuous representation and discrete generation to co-adapt, while Flow
Matching prior learning is decoupled from representation learning via stop-gradient. At inference,
the generated text is re-encoded under the fixed span layout, keeping the latent history consistent
with the actual text context. Systematic component analyses and controlled comparisons on five
downstream tasks with about 210B training tokens validate the core designs and show competitive,
unsaturated scaling. With only 346.03B source tokens, the unconverged checkpoint reaches a
60.10 eight-task average and surpasses Llama-3-8B on HellaSwag and StoryCloze despite its over
43× larger reported pretraining budget. These results indicate effective transfer of the Qweninitialized causal decoder’s generation capability and substantial scaling headroom for hierarchical
language generation. More broadly, continuous-latent hierarchical modeling can combine effectively
with established token-space generation paradigms, including autoregressive and masked-diffusion
decoders.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have made remarkable progress under the autoregressive (AR) paradigm [6, 43],
but the fixed left-to-right generation order imposes an inherent serial dependency on inference. Diffusion
language models (DLMs) [1, 2, 34, 41] offer a new way to break this constraint. Cola DLM [13] further
decomposes text generation into semantic organization in a continuous latent space and text realization in
a discrete space; through latent prior modeling and conditional decoding, it provides a viable path toward
hierarchical language generation beyond direct token modeling.
However, a structural division between continuous and discrete spaces does not automatically yield an effective
division of labor. The key questions are what information the latent space should compress and how the
discrete generator should use it. Shortening the latent sequence is not equivalent to obtaining a compact
semantic representation, and a reconstruction-only objective does not guarantee that the latents can predict
missing or future text [19, 24, 29]. Meanwhile, the decoder must not only faithfully realize the latent semantics,
but also generate the remaining content from the text context when the latent condition is incomplete or
imprecise [44]. Semantic compression and discrete generation therefore cannot be designed independently:
the information the latents should retain must be determined jointly with the information the decoder can
complete.
This interdependence further raises the question of how continuous and discrete representations should be
learned jointly. Fixing the encoder in advance determines the information carried by the latents before the
discrete generator is fully learned, limiting the continual adaptation of the representation to decoding needs.
At the same time, letting the latent-prior fitting objective directly shape the encoder may push the latents
toward directions that are easier to fit but not necessarily more beneficial for discrete generation. Hierarchical
language modeling thus requires not only learning continuous representations and a discrete generator, but
also specifying which objectives should jointly shape the representation and which learning processes should
remain decoupled.
To address these issues, we propose Cola2, which jointly studies continuous semantic compression, the discrete
reconstruction–generation balance, and their co-adaptation within a unified hierarchical language modeling
framework. Its core idea is that, under limited latent capacity, the actual demands of text reconstruction and
generation jointly determine what the continuous representation should retain, while the discrete generator
handles the residual uncertainty that the latents do not explain. Specifically, Cola2 dynamically segments text
into semantic spans, controls the compression rate with an auxiliary-loss-free negative-feedback mechanism,
and combines reconstruction and generation supervision to learn compact and predictive latent representations.
On the discrete side, a block-causal masked diffusion model (MDM) decoder [1, 41] is jointly trained under
clean, noisy, and dropped latent conditions, balancing semantic reconstruction with context-based autonomous
generation, and classifier-free guidance (CFG) [15] adjusts this balance at inference.
During training, the token-prediction loss is backpropagated through the latents to the encoder, so that the
continuous representation and discrete generation co-adapt; the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) [38] learns the
current latent distribution via Flow Matching [26] without backpropagating gradients to the encoder, thereby
decoupling prior fitting from representation learning. At inference, the model re-encodes the generated text
block by block under the fixed span layout, keeping the latent history consistent with the actual text context
and aligning the two spaces throughout generation.
Building on these ideas, we systematically study co-adaptive hierarchical language generation across continuous
and discrete spaces from three perspectives: probabilistic modeling, representation learning, and generation
performance. Our main contributions are as follows:
• We propose Cola2, a co-adaptive hierarchical language modeling framework. From the perspective of
conditional information decomposition, we analyze semantic compression, the reconstruction–generation
balance, and continuous–discrete co-adaptation in a unified manner, clarifying the relation between the
information retained by the latents and the residual left to discrete generation.
• We develop a generation-oriented joint learning mechanism for continuous and discrete representations.
It combines dynamic semantic compression, generation-oriented supervision, and training under latent

Related Work

2.1

Semantic Compression and Hierarchical Language Models

Fixed-granularity methods shorten the modeled sequence through chunking or downsampling [33, 48]. Adaptive
compression methods dynamically aggregate bytes or tokens according to content [18, 36, 39]. Concept-level
methods organize generation through sentence representations or concept prediction [3, 7]. In contrast, Cola2
maintains the target compression rate through negative-feedback control without an auxiliary compression-rate
loss, and combines reconstruction and generation supervision to learn compact and predictive continuous
semantic representations.

2.2

Token-Space Language Models

Autoregressive methods generate text serially, token by token [6, 11, 45]. Discrete diffusion methods predict
multiple positions in parallel through iterative denoising [2, 27, 34, 41, 42, 47], and have been further extended
to block-level generation and token editing [1, 4, 5]. Continuous-augmented methods use paired continuous
states to assist discrete denoising [44, 51, 52]. In contrast, Cola2 learns a semantic prior in a compressed
latent space and then performs conditional generation with a discrete decoder, handling semantic modeling
and text realization hierarchically.

2.3

Continuous Representations and Latent-Space Language Models

Token-aligned methods perform diffusion or flow modeling on continuous representations [8, 10, 12, 16, 22,
23, 46]. Autoencoder-based latent-space methods separate latent generation from text decoding [28, 29, 50].
Joint-learning methods further co-optimize the latent space and the generative model [13, 30]. In contrast,
Cola2 drives representation learning with discrete reconstruction and generation supervision while isolating
the prior-fitting gradient, so that semantic compression and the decoder’s reconstruction–generation balance
are optimized jointly.

3

Cola2: Continuous Latent Diffusion for Hierarchical Text Modeling

We formulate hierarchical text generation as a continuous latent-variable model and, from an informationdecomposition perspective, discuss semantic compression, the reconstruction–generation balance, and continuous–
discrete co-adaptation (Section 3.1). We then present the unified model, training procedure, latent-feedback
inference procedure, and computational cost (Sections 3.2–3.3).

compressed latent space that is semantically rich and easy to model; (2) how to train the latent generator;

generation.

Settings. In the component analyses and controlled scaling experiments, we use the OLMo 2 (dolma2)

tokenizer and the AdamW optimizer with β = (0.9, 0.95), weight decay 0.01, and a maximum sequence length
of 512. Stage 1 jointly trains the encoder and decoder; Stage 2 trains the DiT while continuing to update
the VAE, and blocks the Flow Matching gradient from reaching the encoder. The learning rates of the
Stage 1 VAE and the Stage 2 DiT both start at 10−6 , increase linearly over 5,000 warmup steps to 10−4
and 1.5 × 10−4 , respectively, and then follow cosine decay to 10−6 at step 50,000 and 10−5 at step 1,000,000,
respectively. In Stage 2, the VAE learning rate continues from Stage 1 with a 5,000-step transition, and the
encoder learning rate is 0.1× that of the decoder. Unless otherwise stated, each latent covers 2 tokens on
average, the latent dimension is 64, and the maximum span length is 8. DiT noise times follow a logit-normal
distribution (loc = 2.0, scale = 1.0); the decoder samples clean, noisy, and dropped latent conditions per block
with probabilities 0.2, 0.4, and 0.4, respectively. Inference uses 8 DiT denoising steps and 4 MDM demasking
steps by default, and the controlled scaling experiments train on about 210B tokens.

4.2.4

Generative Supervision of the Encoder

Lemma 4.1 (Generative Supervision Promotes Predictive Representations). When the supervised tokens are
hidden from the encoder, the generative objective cannot be satisfied by copying the current targets. When the
encoder is trainable, reducing this objective encourages the latent to retain information predictive of missing
or future tokens. If the encoder is frozen, the objective only improves the decoder’s use of information already
present in the latent. See Appendix B for the proof.
Obs. 6 Generative supervision is key; its form is secondary. Pure reconstruction reaches only 15.7%, and

adding the Span-AR or BERT-style objective raises accuracy to 25.2% and 26.5%, respectively (Figure 6).
With a frozen encoder, the BERT-style objective yields a 2.2-point gain (28.9% → 31.1%), and unfreezing the
encoder reaches 35.2%. Consistent with Lemma 4.1, the generative objective improves both the completion
ability of the decoder and the latent itself.
Obs. 7 Encoder and decoder should co-evolve. With the MDM decoder, a frozen encoder yields 47.6%,

end-to-end co-evolution raises accuracy to 53.5%, and further adding the generative loss reaches 58.2%
(Figure 6, right). What the latent should retain must be determined jointly with what the decoder can
complete.

4.3.1

VAE Update Policy and Gradient Detachment

Lemma 4.2 (Prior Fitting and Representation Learning Should Be Decoupled). The DiT Flow Matching
objective only requires the current latent to be easier for the prior to fit; its gradient is not guaranteed to align
with the token-side representation objective. Applying stop-gradient to the DiT path ensures that prior fitting
does not directly rewrite the encoder, while preserving end-to-end encoder updates from the decoder token loss.
See Appendix C for the proof.
Obs. 8 Keep training the VAE, but detach the DiT gradient. A trainable VAE reaches 32.8%, whereas a frozen
VAE reaches only 28.7% (Figure 7, left). Allowing the DiT gradient to flow into the encoder yields a 2.2-point
lead early on, but this is overtaken after 80k steps and becomes a 1.0-point deficit at 140k steps (Table 2).
This supports Lemma 4.2: the DiT gradient rewrites the latent to be easier to fit rather than more useful for
text modeling, and detaching the gradient blocks this latent hacking.

4.3.2

Noise-Schedule Calibration

Lemma 4.3 (Effective Noise Is Jointly Determined by the Latent and Diffusion). At the same DiT timestep,
the available latent signal is affected by both VAE posterior uncertainty and diffusion corruption. Consequently,
changing the noise scale of the Stage 1 latent changes the effective signal-to-noise ratio induced by the same
DiT schedule, so the noise schedule must be calibrated jointly with the latent distribution. See Appendix D for
the proof.
Obs. 9 The noise schedule must match the representation. All three configurations achieve their best results at

loc = 2.0, and deviating from it costs 0.3–3.0 points, with the magnitude varying with dz and the compression
level (Figure 7, right). By Lemma 4.3, the schedule should be recalibrated when the representation changes.

4.4

Balancing the Reconstruction--Generation Decoder

Lemma 4.4 (Latent-Condition Strength Determines Residual Generation). Given the same discrete context,
a more reliable latent condition provides more information and leaves less residual uncertainty for the decoder
to generate on its own; when the latent is corrupted or removed, the residual uncertainty increases accordingly.
Thus, mixed cond/ noise/ drop training covers the continuum from conditional reconstruction to autonomous
generation. See Appendix E for the proof.
4.4.1

Decoder Type

Obs. 12 Decoder factorization matters little. At every checkpoint, the gap between them is at most 1.4 points,

and the leader alternates during training (Table 3): AR-MTP leads by 0.9 points at 50k steps, whereas MDM
leads by 0.7 and 1.4 points at 100k and 150k steps. This indicates that, given a shared latent–token interface,
the specific decoder factorization is not the decisive factor for performance, and the hierarchical framework
applies to both; subsequent experiments use the MDM decoder.

4.6

Summary

The results support the design of Cola2 in three respects. Semantic compression: dynamic segmentation
and generative supervision keep the latents strongly semantic at high compression rates; the former slightly
surpasses the uncompressed model with an appropriate Lmax , and the latter raises accuracy from 15.7% to
26.7%. Reconstruction--generation balance: mixed training dominated by noisy and dropped conditions
enables the decoder both to faithfully use the latent (shuffling the latent costs 12.2 points) and to autonomously
generate the residual information. Continuous--discrete co-adaptation: the information placed in the latent
should be determined by the decoder; co-training the encoder and decoder yields a 5.9-point gain, and both
continue to benefit from more training data, giving the hierarchical model scaling trends comparable to or
better than AR and LLaDA.

5

Limitations & Future Prospects

Although our results provide initial evidence for the feasibility, competitiveness, and scaling potential of Cola2,
we view the current system as a starting point rather than a finished endpoint. First, the model remains far
from convergence, especially the latent DiT, while the present training scale is limited by available resources.
Larger models, longer training, and broader data and compute budgets are therefore needed to establish its
performance ceiling. Second, compression reduces the dominant linear and attention FLOPs of the latent prior
in theory, but end-to-end generation speed also depends on the decoder, CFG branches, latent feedback, and
hardware utilization. Infrastructure co-design—including specialized kernels, cache management, and parallel
scheduling—is needed to translate theoretical savings into practical speedups. Third, Cola2 already shows
compatibility with autoregressive and masked-diffusion decoders, but their co-training objectives, inference
trade-offs, and optimal division of labor remain underexplored. Finally, the continuous latent space may
provide a shared interface between text and other continuous modalities such as vision, opening a path toward
unified latent representations, cross-modal priors, and unified understanding and generation.

6

Conclusion

We presented Cola2, a hierarchical language modeling framework that unifies dynamic semantic compression
with balanced discrete reconstruction and generation through continuous–discrete co-adaptation. Dynamic
compression and generation-oriented supervision encourage compact, predictive latent representations, while
the decoder realizes latent semantics and handles residual uncertainty from context. Token-prediction gradients
drive encoder–decoder co-adaptation, whereas latent-prior fitting remains decoupled from representation
learning. Systematic component analyses and controlled evaluations on five downstream tasks, scaled to
about 210B training tokens, support these design principles and show competitive, unsaturated scaling. In a
separate resource-constrained run, the final checkpoint trained on 346.03B source tokens achieves a 60.10
average over eight tasks and surpasses Llama-3-8B on HellaSwag and StoryCloze, although that baseline
reports over 43× more pretraining tokens. These results indicate successful transfer of the Qwen-initialized
causal decoder’s generation capability and substantial scaling headroom, especially because the latent DiT
remains far from convergence. More broadly, Cola2 shows that continuous-latent hierarchical modeling can
integrate effectively with established token-space generators, including autoregressive and masked-diffusion
decoders, rather than depending on a single decoder paradigm.

Final-Checkpoint Pretraining Recipe

This section describes the large-scale recipe used for the final checkpoint in Table 4. It is separate from the
matched component and controlled scaling setup in Section 4.1.

I.1

Shared Training Configuration

The pretraining corpus uses OLMo 2 pretraining data retokenized with the Qwen3-4B tokenizer. The sequence
length is 4096.
All stages use AdamW with β = (0.9, 0.95) and weight decay 0.01. The EMA decay in Stages II and III is
0.9998. At stage boundaries, model parameters, optimizer state, the learning-rate scheduler, and the data
cursor are restored.

I.2

Stage I: Dynamic-Compression VAE

Stage I processes approximately 52.43B source tokens. Dynamic segmentation compresses about two tokens
into one latent on average, with maximum span length 8 and latent dimension 64. The decoder is initialized
from Qwen3-4B-Base, and the VAE encoder embedding is copied from the same model; the remaining
compression modules are initialized from scratch.
The Qwen-initialized parameters remain frozen for the first 5.24B source tokens and are subsequently updated
at 0.1× the main learning rate; the compression encoder also uses a 0.1× multiplier. The learning rate is
linearly warmed up from 10−6 to 10−4 over the first 5.24B source tokens and then cosine-decayed to 10−6 .
The decoder uses a causal dual-stream objective. Masked positions are supplied as [MASK] inputs, but the
decoder self-attention remains causal rather than becoming bidirectional. Clean, noisy, and dropped latent
conditions are sampled with proportions 0.4/0.3/0.3, and their three token-loss weights are all 1. The KL,
duration, and encoder-mask auxiliary loss weights are 10−4 , 0.1, and 1, respectively, and the encoder-mask
span ratio is 0.15.

I.3

Stage II: Joint VAE and Latent-DiT Training

Stage II restores the complete VAE checkpoint, optimizer, scheduler, and data cursor from Stage I. It introduces
an approximately 4B-parameter latent DiT with 34 Transformer layers, hidden dimension 2560, 20 attention
heads, and latent block size 8. This stage processes approximately 125.83B source tokens.
The VAE continues to use the Stage-I causal dual-stream objective, with the encoder-mask span ratio increased
to 0.30. The DiT reads detached VAE latents, so its diffusion loss does not backpropagate into the VAE; the
VAE is updated only by its compression and decoder objectives. The DiT uses velocity-prediction MSE, with
timesteps sampled from LogitNormal(loc = 2, scale = 1).
The DiT learning rate is warmed up from 10−6 to 1.5 × 10−4 and then cosine-decayed. The schedule is defined
over a nominal 2.1T-token horizon, although training terminates before reaching that horizon.

I.4

Stage III: Frozen VAE and Continued DiT Training

Stage III restores the complete DiT parameters, EMA, optimizer, learning-rate scheduler, and data cursor
from the Stage-II checkpoint. The Stage-II EMA VAE encoder is frozen, and only the DiT continues to be
optimized. The released model uses the matching EMA VAE decoder associated with this encoder.
The timestep distribution remains LogitNormal(loc = 2, scale = 1). This stage processes approximately
167.77B source tokens from the retokenized OLMo 2 corpus.
