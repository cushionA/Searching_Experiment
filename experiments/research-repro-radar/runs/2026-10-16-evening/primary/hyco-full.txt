We present HyCo , which assigns depth to the server model’s native multi-token prediction (MTP) head, executed on the device, and alternative branches to one SLM pass. The server returns the hidden state, input embedding, and key–value entries needed by the head instead of executing its sequential steps. Quantization, a restricted vocabulary, and a 1K-token window fit both drafters to the NPU. An ϵ \epsilon -threshold rule additionally relaxes exact preservation of the verifier’s sampling distribution. In single-client runs with a Galaxy S26 Ultra over LTE/5G, HyCo reduces server GPU time per output token relative to server-side MTP self-speculation by 38.6% on Spec-Bench and 29.2% on 32K-token summarization. With the same acceptance rule, three Spec-Bench runs per system show 9.0 percentage points more saving and 1.9 × 1.9\times delivery speed than an SLM-only drafter. HyCo differs from Cloud+MTP by + 0.2 +0.2 judge-score points (paired 95% CI: − 0.9 -0.9 to + 1.3 +1.3 ); exploratory translation favors SpecEdge- ϵ \epsilon . Without relaxation, HyCo exact saves 27.7% and 11.9% on the two datasets. 1 Introduction Speculative decoding accelerates large language models (LLMs) by verifying several draft tokens in one forward pass, with exact verification preserving the LLM’s sampling distribution ( Leviathan et al., 2023 ; Chen et al., 2023 ) . Serving engines support it ( Kwon et al., 2023 ; Zheng et al., 2024 ) , and recent LLMs include a native multi-token prediction (MTP) head ( DeepSeek-AI, 2024 ; Gemma Team, 2026 ) , one of several approaches to drafting from the LLM’s hidden states ( Cai et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2024 ; Li et al., 2025b ) . Device–cloud speculative decoding instead places a small language model (SLM) on the user’s device and leaves verification to the server ( Park et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025a ) . Communication can make an individual request slower than server-only speculation ( Lyu et al., 2026 ) ; the opportunity is therefore to reduce server computation without making the mobile experience impractically slow. We target two goals. The server should spend less GPU time per output token than Cloud+MTP , which already runs the LLM’s own MTP self-speculation. The user should receive tokens at least as fast as the SLM alone generates them on the device. These are distinct reference points: Cloud+MTP measures the server-side opportunity, whereas the SLM alone represents a local alternative for the user. Meeting both goals is difficult on a mobile neural processing unit (NPU). The drafters must fit integer execution graphs, limited shared memory, and compiled context lengths. Acceptance varies across tasks ( Liu et al., 2024a ; Xia et al., 2024 ) and determines how effectively each round amortizes server work and communication. A tree offers alternative tokens at each position and is verified in one pass ( Miao et al., 2024 ) , but growing it with an autoregressive SLM requires a sequential pass per level. The four-level SpecEdge-style tree studied here raises the mean number of tokens accepted per round (MAT), yet its four mobile SLM passes leave the user slower than the SLM alone. The challenge is to obtain useful depth and alternatives without repeatedly running the SLM. We present HyCo , based on a division of drafting work. The LLM’s native MTP head reads only a small slice of the LLM’s state, so it can execute on the mobile device. This MTP Drafter supplies a chain that gives the tree its depth. The server’s LLM, the verifier , returns the head’s inputs with each verification rather than executing the head’s sequential steps itself. One pass of the device’s SLM Drafter adds alternatives under the root and every planted node, allowing the tree to continue where the chain breaks. Together, the two form the Hybrid Drafter , whose output is the hybrid draft tree . The MTP head still runs sequentially, but depth no longer requires extra SLM passes. Figure 1: HyCo on Spec-Bench. HyCo : three-run mean; other configurations: one run. Delivery speed: tokens/s from a request’s start to its last token. Integer quantization, a restricted output vocabulary, and a 1K-token window fit both drafters to the NPU, at some cost in acceptance. On the server, ϵ \epsilon -threshold acceptance additionally accepts drafted tokens whose probability under the verifier’s sampling distribution reaches ϵ \epsilon ( Hewitt et al., 2022 ) . This raises acceptance but changes the output distribution. We therefore evaluate quality alongside server GPU time, mobile draft time, and user delivery speed, and compare both an exact-verification ablation and an SLM-only tree under the same threshold. We evaluate Gemma 4 26B-A4B on an RTX PRO 6000 cloud GPU, with its MTP head and Gemma 4 E2B on a Galaxy S26 Ultra over LTE/5G. In single-client measurements, HyCo reduces server GPU time per output token against Cloud+MTP by 38.6% on Spec-Bench ( Xia et al., 2024 , SB;) and 29.2% on 32K-token summarization from ∞ \infty Bench Sum ( Zhang et al., 2024 ) . On SB, three runs of both HyCo and the SLM-only baseline under the same ϵ \epsilon rule give reductions of 38.6 ± 0.2 38.6\pm 0.2 % and 29.6 ± 0.2 29.6\pm 0.2 %, respectively. HyCo delivers 35.0 tokens/s, 1.9 × 1.9\times the baseline’s rate and faster than the SLM alone. With exact verification, HyCo still saves 27.7% and 11.9%, respectively. Repeated generation and long-summary assessment characterize the quality trade-offs. Our contribution is the native-head/SLM division of drafting work and an implementation demonstrating its usefulness under mobile NPU constraints. 2 Related Work Work on device–cloud speculative decoding either strengthens the draft or skips verification where the device is confident. The draft is strengthened in three ways: drafting a tree instead of a sequence ( Miao et al., 2024 ; Park et al., 2025 ) , training the draft model toward the target ( Zhou et al., 2024 ) , or drafting several tokens in parallel ( An et al., 2026 ) . The latter two retrain the draft model and are orthogonal to our approach: HyCo keeps its drafters as released, apart from quantization and a restricted output vocabulary, and strengthens the draft through its tree. In SLM-based device–cloud systems, the device’s draft model drafts every proposed token ( Park et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025a ; Li et al., 2026c ) , one sequential pass per tree level, and SpecEdge overlaps these passes with the round trip by proactive drafting (PD), preparing the next round’s tree while the reply is in flight. Among the device–cloud systems in Table 1 , none drafts with the LLM’s native draft head: HAT ( Xie et al., 2026 ) runs the LLM’s first layers on the device, whereas Li et al. (2026a) draft from hidden states returned by the server . Cloud+MTP drafts with that head, but on the server. HyCo instead runs that head on the mobile device, beside one SLM pass. Skipping verification by confidence saves rounds but is lossy. Synera keeps on the device the tokens whose confidence clears a threshold and offloads only the rest for the cloud to verify ( Wang et al., 2025 ) . The cloud’s LLM does not check the locally retained tokens. HyCo instead checks every round with the verifier and raises acceptance within that check through ϵ \epsilon -threshold acceptance; its relaxed rule is also lossy. Raising acceptance within verification is the aim of lossy acceptance rules, which accept drafted tokens that the target would not necessarily sample, and they differ in what they test. BiLD keeps a small model’s tokens unless its distribution diverges from the large model’s beyond a threshold ( Kim et al., 2023 ) , and fuzzy speculative decoding and the edge–cloud E 2 -SCI accept within divergence thresholds of their own ( Holsman et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2026b ) . Judge decoding lets a learned judge keep tokens that exact verification rejects ( Bachmann et al., 2025 ) , and Medusa’s typical acceptance tests the target’s raw softmax against an entropy-adaptive threshold taken from η \eta -sampling ( Cai et al., 2024 ; Hewitt et al., 2022 ) . HyCo ’s ϵ \epsilon -threshold acceptance instead thresholds the verifier’s own sampling distribution p ~ \tilde{p} (§ 3.5 ) . 3 Method In HyCo , the mobile device constructs a hybrid draft tree and the server’s verifier checks it (Algorithm 1 ). We measure and compare settings on UC-val, 60 prompts from the test split of UltraChat 200k ( Ding et al., 2023 ; Tunstall et al., 2024 , UC;) , and fix the chosen configuration for the reported SB and ∞ \infty Bench Sum runs (Appendix D ) . Server cost g g is the total GPU time including prefill divided by output tokens. For decoding, this is server time per round divided by MAT. The default selection criterion minimizes g g among settings whose LLM-judge score (§ 4.3 ) falls within the observed lossless-run range, 58.7–63.0. This is a screening heuristic, not a quality-equivalence test: two runs of the same setting scored 57.1 and 61.5 . When g g differs by less than the approximately 2% variation observed in validation, we use the stated device-cost or comparison rationale . 3.1 Hybrid draft tree Figure 2: One HyCo round around its hybrid draft tree. Drawn with at most two of each node’s 32 SLM Drafter candidates and 12 kept nodes. Figure 2 depicts one round, from the verifier’s hand-off to its check of the trimmed tree. The MTP Drafter is the verifier’s own MTP head, quantized by our quantization-aware training (QAT), and drafts each round’s chain of K K top-1 tokens, which gives the hybrid draft tree its depth. It reads the input embedding e ⁡ ( x t + 1 ) e(x_{t+1}) of the tree’s root x t + 1 x_{t+1} , the last hidden state h t h_{t} from which the verifier sampled x t + 1 x_{t+1} , and the keys and values (KV) of two verifier layers . The server returns these inputs with each verification, 5.6 KB per round plus 6.1 KB per output token at 8 bits, and skips the K K sequential steps that its MTP head runs after every verification pass in Cloud+MTP . Running the MTP head on the mobile device instead of the server shortens the server’s median round from 26.8 to 21.7 ms and lowers g g by 15.2%, at about 20 ms more per round on the mobile device . At its first two steps, the MTP Drafter also plants its second-ranked token beside the chain token as a runner-up sibling , in case the top-1 misses the verifier’s choice . The SLM Drafter, Gemma 4 E2B from Google’s QAT checkpoint ( Google DeepMind, 2026 ) , then proposes in one pass children under the root and every planted node, giving the tree its width. Trimming the tree to its node budget needs scores that weigh the chain’s nodes, which carry no probability, against the SLM’s candidates. Each depth along a node’s path costs ln ⁡ 0.66 ≈ − 0.42 \ln 0.66\approx-0.42 , as the chain’s acceptance rate per depth, the share of rounds whose first k k chain tokens are all accepted among those whose first k − 1 k{-}1 are, is 0.65–0.68 at every depth, with a geometric mean of 0.66 . A depth- d d chain node thus scores d ​ ln ⁡ 0.66 d\ln 0.66 , about the logarithm of its chance of being accepted. A runner-up sibling pays this cost twice on its own edge, which ranks it just below the chain token it doubles, and an SLM candidate adds its token’s log-probability to the cost . The tree is then trimmed by score, closed under ancestors, and sent as token IDs and parent indices, 2.2 KB per round . The verifier checks it in one forward pass with a tree attention mask ( Miao et al., 2024 ) . Under exact verification , it samples at each node v v from its sampling distribution p ~ ( ⋅ ∣ v ) \tilde{p}(\cdot\mid v) and descends to the matching child, so the committed text follows p ~ \tilde{p} and verification is lossless. The first sample that matches no child ends the round as its bonus token , which MAT counts and which becomes the root of the next tree. The chain supplies most accepted tokens, and the siblings and the SLM’s alternatives take over where it breaks. An SLM leaf extends a fully accepted chain to a fifth token in 17% of rounds under exact verification . We choose K = 4 K{=}4 and runner-up siblings at depths 1–2 on UC-val. The cumulative chain acceptance reaches 18.9% at depth 4; later siblings bring little additional server saving. The 32-node budget matches SpecEdge-style, and the verifier uses the same 33-row CUDA graphs, including the root. A second SLM pass is retained as the HyCo -2P operating option rather than the default (Appendix D ). 3.2 Quantization Integer graphs let both drafters exploit the mobile NPU ( Tan et al., 2024 ) . In a diagnostic comparison, 16-bit floating-point activations lengthened the SLM Drafter’s 32-row pass from 15.7 to 38–41 ms at unchanged acceptance . The SLM’s QAT checkpoint has 2-, 4-, or 8-bit weights but computes in floating point ( Gemma Team, 2026 ; Google DeepMind, 2026 ) . We convert it through graph surgery and activation calibration without retraining its weights. The verifier’s MTP head is released in bfloat16 (BF16); we apply 4-bit QAT to its linear weights. Both drafters use 16-bit fixed-point activations and static graphs compiled with the Qualcomm AI Runtime ( Qualcomm Technologies, Inc., 2026 , QAIRT;) . For the reported collaborative configurations, activation calibration and head QAT use 60 prompts from UC-tr, the train_sft split of UltraChat 200k, not SB prompts. The head is trained for 100 steps by minimizing Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence from the BF16 head on the verifier’s greedy answers. On UC-val, this raises top-1 agreement with BF16 from 87.4% to 91.0%; 8-bit weights retain 98.8% without training but double weight storage (Appendix A ). 3.3 Restricted output vocabulary The output layers of both drafters span 262,144 tokens, and these layers and their top- k k search, repeated for every row of the SLM pass and every MTP Drafter step, take a large share of each round on the mobile device. The full vocabulary lengthens the mobile device’s time per round from 52.9 to 77.9 ms . As in FR-Spec ( Zhao et al., 2025 ) , both drafters therefore predict from one keep set of 86,089 tokens, mostly the tokens most frequent in the answers of UC-tr, plus the special and byte tokens . The MTP Drafter embeds its own drafted tokens from an 8-bit, 242 MB table of the verifier’s input embeddings for the kept tokens . Neither drafter can propose the tokens outside the keep set, which make up 3.0% of the verifier’s bonus tokens on UC-val, although UC-val is drawn from the keep set’s own dataset, and more on SB . The verifier still samples them from its full vocabulary as bonus tokens, which both drafters take as input, so the restriction costs only acceptance and exact verification stays lossless. By the default criterion, we use the 86K keep set, whose g g of 6.23 ms is the lowest of the four sizes, against 6.44–7.57 ms, as Table 6 in Appendix D shows . The full vocabulary has the highest g g of all, as its MAT is the lowest, 2.85. 3.4 1K-token context window Both drafters read only the last 1,024 tokens of the context, as prior drafters do on long inputs ( Sadhukhan et al., 2025 ; Valliappan, 2026 ) . The SLM Drafter keeps its KV cache as a ring of the last 1,024 committed tokens, or 512 in its sliding-window layers, so the mobile device never prefills more than 1,024 prompt tokens . The MTP Drafter reads the verifier’s keys and values over the same window and prefills nothing on the device, while the verifier prefills the whole prompt. The device’s memory and prefill time set both drafters’ windows at 1,024 tokens, as UC-val’s short prompts cannot tell window lengths apart. An SLM Drafter context of 4,096 tokens instead of 1,024 moves g g from 6.82 to 6.77 ms, and MTP head windows of 512–2,048 tokens, run with the head on the server, give 7.75–7.89 ms, both within noise . 3.5 ϵ \epsilon -threshold acceptance The quantized, restricted and windowed drafters on the mobile device propose some of the verifier’s likely tokens less often than full drafters would, and tokens outside the keep set not at all . Exact verification thus ends rounds that a plausible drafted child could have continued. Under ϵ \epsilon -threshold acceptance , a drafted child c c of node v v is accepted if it matches the verifier’s sample or if p ~ ​ ( c ∣ v ) ≥ ϵ \tilde{p}(c\mid v)\geq\epsilon . Here p ~ \tilde{p} is the verifier’s sampling distribution after temperature scaling and top- k k /top- p p truncation, rather than its raw softmax. With a one-hot greedy distribution, the threshold adds no token beyond exact verification. The fixed threshold follows ϵ \epsilon -sampling ( Hewitt et al., 2022 ) . The verifier selects the deepest path of accepted children and appends its sample at that path’s endpoint as the bonus token . Among equally deep paths, it prefers the one with more verifier-sampled edges, then the highest product of p ~ \tilde{p} . This is a path-level preference, not a requirement to retain every sampled edge. Every accepted token is in the support of p ~ \tilde{p} , but the rule can commit tokens that the verifier did not sample and changes their selection probabilities. It is therefore lossy: membership in the verifier’s sampling support does not guarantee distribution preservation or equivalent quality. These reference-based measures add evidence about summary content but are not a guarantee of factual consistency. A fixed ± 20 \pm 20 -point screen of HyCo ’s three-run per-question means against Cloud+MTP selects 13 decreases and 12 increases. Our manual inspection identifies six substantive errors among the decreases, as well as score/content discrepancies in both directions. We retain all measured scores; the selected cases are not an estimate of the overall error rate (Appendix F ). 4.6 Mobile costs and head transfer On SB, HyCo delivers 35.0 tokens/s versus 26.4 for the SLM alone. Its 12.1 ms of mobile draft time per output token is about a third of the SLM alone’s 36.8 ms. SpecEdge-style exact delivers 16.1 tokens/s and consumes 42.8 ms of mobile draft time per token. The two efficiency goals therefore use distinct references: less server work than Cloud+MTP, and faster delivery than the local SLM. Since Cloud+MTP was not streamed to the phone, we do not compare its user delivery speed with HyCo . In a separate paired 150-question experiment, the device-head configuration (W4, 86K keep set, 1K window) has 4.0% lower MAT than the original BF16 server head (95% CI: 3.0–4.9%). In the full SB experiment, relaxing HyCo exact raises MAT from 3.84 to 4.71. These are separate comparisons, not additive estimates of independent component effects. The first hand-off introduces a startup cost. Single-run HyCo traces have mean time to first token of 846 ms on SB and 4.60 s on 32K inputs. Thus, higher average delivery speed does not imply a lower first-token delay. We report GPU-busy efficiency, not loaded-service capacity or energy savings (Appendix G ). 5 Discussion 5.1 From server accelerator to client drafter Device–cloud systems commonly offload an independent draft model ( Park et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025a ; Li et al., 2026c ) . When the server already has a native draft head, the remaining server-side opportunity can be small ( Lyu et al., 2026 ) . HyCo changes the work split itself: it moves the native head’s sequential steps to the mobile device and returns the slice of verifier state that the head reads. MTP self-speculation becomes a device–cloud protocol rather than only a server-internal accelerator. This split is useful when server GPU time is a priority and the head’s inputs fit the link. The UC-val comparison in § 3.1 exchanges about 5 ms of server time per round for about 20 ms of additional mobile work, while the independent SLM supplies alternative branches without taking over the chain’s depth . Our single-client measurements establish the execution-time trade-off, not the capacity gain of a loaded service. 5.2 Choosing the mobile–cloud work split The configurations expose different operating points. Prior systems adapt draft length to the channel ( Li et al., 2026c ) , an acceptance threshold ( Li et al., 2026b ) , or which tokens reach the server ( Wang et al., 2025 ) . Here, the choices also include where the native head executes and how many SLM passes accompany it. Figure 1 illustrates the associated deployment priorities, rather than an evaluated online switching policy. More drafting is not uniformly beneficial. The second SLM pass in HyCo -2P exchanges more mobile work for a larger server saving, at 29.0 tokens/s on SB-480 against 35.0 for default HyCo . On ∞ \infty Bench Sum, however, that pass adds only 2.7 percentage points of server saving and reduces delivery speed by 10%: its MAT gain is smaller, and prefill, which drafting does not shorten, accounts for about a fifth of g g . Cloud+MTP removes mobile drafting altogether. Among the exact configurations, server head uses 9.2 ms of mobile draft time per output token versus 14.7 ms for HyCo exact, with 13.1 percentage points less server saving on SB . The preferred split depends on which resource is constrained; time measurements alone do not determine an energy-optimal choice. 5.3 Lossy acceptance revisited Wang et al. (2026) identify risks of truncation-based verification: an allowed set constrains which tokens can be accepted, but does not preserve the verifier’s relative preferences within that set. Our ϵ \epsilon -threshold acceptance belongs to this category. The repeated and long-summary results in § 4.5 characterize its trade-off on the evaluated tasks; they do not remove the underlying distributional risk. The exact comparisons favor HyCo over the SLM-only baseline on both SB and 32K inputs, while the same- ϵ \epsilon comparisons retain an efficiency advantage after matching the relaxed rule. The latter controls the acceptance rule but not the proposal distribution. Its overall score interval and translation difference support a task-dependent trade-off. The fixed-tree UC-val replay of § 3.5 helps explain which proposals benefit. About two thirds of threshold-only accepted tokens come from the verifier’s MTP chain, a tenth from runner-up siblings, and a quarter from the SLM . Thus, most such tokens follow the native head’s proposals rather than an independent drafter’s. This composition, the use of p ~ \tilde{p} rather than raw softmax, and the path-level preference for sampled edges are plausible explanations for the observed scores, not guarantees of quality. The replay follows fixed logged trajectories and does not measure the quality of newly generated answers. 6 Conclusion HyCo combines two complementary drafters on a mobile NPU: the verifier’s native MTP head supplies depth, and one SLM pass supplies alternative branches. Returning the head’s inputs instead of executing its sequential steps reduces server work, while the hybrid tree avoids repeated SLM passes for depth. Quantization, a restricted vocabulary, and a bounded context make this split practical on the device. In single-client measurements, HyCo with ϵ \epsilon -threshold acceptance reduces server GPU time per output token against Cloud+MTP by 38.6% on SB and 29.2% on 32K-token summarization; with exact verification, it still saves 27.7% and 11.9%, respectively. On SB, it delivers tokens 32.5% faster than the SLM alone at a third of its mobile draft time per output token. Exact-verification and same- ϵ \epsilon comparisons support the design, while repeated generation and summary assessment quantify a task-dependent quality–efficiency trade-off. Hybrid drafting therefore offers a practical mobile–cloud operating point without requiring the SLM alone to provide both depth and alternatives. Applying the same split to other model families is a natural next step; it requires a native head that the mobile device can run, and Appendix H shows the SLM Drafter working for Muse-Glimmer-30B through a token bridge without retraining. Limitations Deployment scope and cost. Each round waits for one network round trip, which bounds mobile delivery speed. Retaining unverified device tokens, as in Synera ( Wang et al., 2025 ) , would introduce a different lossy mechanism. The full hybrid deployment uses one mobile device, one commercial cellular network, one cloud GPU type, and one verifier family. All configurations serve one client at a time in our engine. We measure GPU execution time and mobile draft time, not loaded-service capacity, monetary cost, or device and radio energy. Distribution and quality. Default HyCo does not preserve the verifier’s sampling distribution. The reported judge-score interval permits a modest quality decrease. The aggregate covers scored non-QA first-turn answers, not QA or second-turn quality. Translation favors SpecEdge- ϵ \epsilon in an exploratory comparison. The question-only judge and reference-summary-based HELMET metrics do not comprehensively assess factual consistency, and we do not establish how relaxed acceptance changes output diversity. Only the two main relaxed systems have three SB generations; the other configurations and Cloud+MTP have one. Question-bootstrap intervals condition on these outputs, and one judge evaluation per output does not separate judging from generation variability. The UC-val screening range is not an equivalence margin. Appendices A and D distinguish calibration and development data from the reported benchmark runs. Requirements and device costs. HyCo requires access to the verifier’s native head, intermediate hidden states, and KV entries, which may not be available from closed model services; its head must also run on the mobile device, and the SLM needs a shared tokenizer or token bridge. Heads that read more verifier layers ( Chen et al., 2026 ) can require a larger hand-off. The MTP Drafter adds about 20 ms per mobile round and up to 6.3 MB to the first reply of a long prompt. The device stores 365 MB for it alongside the SLM’s 754 MB graph . Vocabulary, context, and input scope. The UC-derived keep set and 1K window need not suit every language or domain ( Abramovich et al., 2026 ) . Tokens outside the keep set comprise 3.0% of verifier bonus tokens on UC-val and 6.8% on the 150-question SB subset of the head-configuration comparison (§ 4.6 ) . Exact verification preserves the output distribution despite these proposal restrictions; relaxed acceptance does not. The SLM accesses earlier context only indirectly through the MTP chain. We evaluate text only, not Gemma’s image or audio inputs. Information exposure. Each client holds the MTP head and part of the verifier’s input embeddings. Transmitted activations and reply-size patterns may expose information beyond the text ( Cunningham, 2026 ) . We evaluate no safeguard. References Abramovich et al. (2026) T. Abramovich, M. Appendix B Verifier Engine We ported the verifier’s text model with static shapes into SpecEdge’s server ( Park et al., 2025 ) , replacing its model side, and checked it against the Hugging Face model . Attention projections run as 4-bit integer Marlin matrix products ( Frantar et al., 2025 ) and the experts, 8 of 128 per token, through vLLM’s fused Marlin mixture-of-experts kernel ( Kwon et al., 2023 ) , while the dense MLP and the router stay in 16 bits. RMS normalization runs in FlashInfer ( Ye et al., 2025 ) , and attention in PyTorch ( Paszke et al., 2019 ) with the tree mask. Tree verification uses the routed MoE kernel described above rather than a dense all-expert path. Each decode round is padded to the narrowest of ten attention windows that covers its context, 256, 512 and 768 positions and then powers of two up to the maximum context of 65,536, and replays that window’s CUDA graphs, while prefill runs eagerly in chunks . The verifier’s MTP head, which Cloud+MTP and the server head variant run on the server, replays its own graphs over the same windows, and its chains agree with eager execution on 99.2–99.6% of tokens, as often as two stock attention kernels agree. Appendix H Additional Cross-Family Measurements The SLM half of the Hybrid Drafter is not tied to Gemma 4. In additional runs, our SLM Drafter drafted without retraining on a Galaxy Z Fold8 smartphone, which shares the S26 Ultra’s SM8850 chipset, for Muse-Glimmer-30B in NVFP4 ( Meta Superintelligence Lab, 2026 ; NVIDIA, 2026 ) , a verifier of another family whose tokenizer it is mapped to through a token bridge. Glimmer’s own DFlash2 head ( Chen et al., 2026 ; Inco AI, 2026 ) , which reads five of its layers and was not deployed on the device, drafted eight-token chains on the server, as in our server head variant . Over all 480 SB questions, a SpecEdge-style tree of four SLM passes and 127 nodes (128 verifier rows, against 33 in Table 5 ) reached an MAT of 3.16, and a 32-node tree of the chain and a single SLM pass reached 4.07. These runs used another verifier, server GPU, and engine, and answers of up to 2,048 tokens, so they are not comparable with Table 2 , but they show that the SLM Drafter carries over to another model family through a token bridge. A head that the mobile device can run for such families would move the whole Hybrid Drafter, not only its SLM half, to the device, which we leave to future work.
