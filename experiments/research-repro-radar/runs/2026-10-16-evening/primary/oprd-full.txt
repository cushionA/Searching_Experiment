Abstract Reliable repository-level coding requires agents not only to generate correct code but also to preserve constraints and respond to tool feedback across long interactions. However, our diagnostic analysis shows that agents often fail to act on relevant information already available in context, while final Pass/Fail outcomes provide only sparse process supervision. We introduce On-Policy Reminder Distillation (OPRD) , which combines Multi-Scope Reminder supervision with outcome-based routing on reminder-free student rollouts. Successful rollouts provide autoregressive self-imitation targets, whereas failed rollouts receive Reminder-conditioned reverse-KL supervision from a teacher that re-evaluates the same student-generated prefixes. A Global Reminder converts final-test diagnostics into trajectory-level guidance, while Turn-local Reminders apply only to the next decision following the relevant environment observation. The teacher and student share a two-stage initialization from Coding SFT and Instruction-Following (IF) SFT. At inference, the student operates without Reminders. On SWE-bench Verified , SWE-bench Pro , and SWE-bench Multilingual , OPRD improves the benchmark-averaged task resolution rate by 11.3 points over the open-source Qwen3.5-4B base model. Under the same diagnostic protocol, it reduces the aggregate error-event count from 446 to 222, a 50.2% reduction. 1 Tsinghua University, 2 Independent Researcher 3 Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, 4 National University of Singapore luo-yc24@mails.tsinghua.edu.cn 1 Introduction Figure 1: Diagnostic analysis of Qwen3.5-4B on 200 tasks from the SWE-rebench v2 validation set. Large language models have advanced in reasoning ( Zhan et al. 2025a ; Deng et al. 2025a ; Xu et al. 2026b ; Miao et al. 2026 ) and repository-level software engineering ( Jimenez et al. 2024 ; Yang et al. 2024 ; Zhang et al. 2024 ; Ouyang et al. 2025 ; Xia et al. 2025 ) . Reliable long-horizon coding nevertheless requires agents to preserve task constraints and adapt to tool and execution feedback ( Yao et al. 2023 ; Chen et al. 2024 ) . We examine this challenge on 200 SWE-rebench V2 validation tasks ( Badertdinov et al. 2026 ) , using two Qwen3.5-4B rollouts per task under identical inference settings ( Qwen Team 2026 ) . In the reminder-free rollout, deterministic rules identify explicit tool and execution failures, while GPT-5.4 classifies remaining context-dependent events from the task and complete trajectory ( OpenAI 2026 ) . Each event belongs to one of four categories in Table 6 : Tool-Feedback Recovery , Scope and Source Grounding , Scenario-Condition Reasoning , and Post-Edit Control and Validation . In the second rollout, the agent restarts from the task under the same settings, and the Reminder mechanism examines each environment observation to decide whether to insert a Turn-local Reminder before the next action. Applying the same annotation protocol reveals fewer error events across all four categories (Fig. 1 ). Post-hoc annotation audits completed trajectories, while runtime Reminder selection uses only the currently visible interaction prefix. This finding suggests that reliable long-horizon coding requires models to preserve task-level constraints across a trajectory and use local interaction feedback at appropriate decision points. A natural direction is therefore to provide process-level corrective information with explicit temporal scope. However, coding-agent training relies on supervised imitation of successful trajectories or executable-outcome optimization ( Pan et al. 2025 ; Jain et al. 2025 ; Wei et al. 2025 ) . These signals improve task completion but do not indicate which constraints should persist or which observation should guide the next decision. At inference time, agents can adapt to feedback ( Yao et al. 2023 ; Chen et al. 2024 ) or recover missed information through reflection and self-critique ( Shinn et al. 2023 ; Madaan et al. 2023 ; Gou et al. 2024 ) . Runtime reminders require deployment-time construction and injection, consume context, and tie behavior to an agent scaffold. On-policy distillation trains students on self-generated outputs to reduce train–inference mismatch ( Agarwal et al. 2024 ; Gu et al. 2024 ) . Related work uses context- and feedback-conditioned distillation to internalize behavior induced by auxiliary instructions or environment feedback ( Ye et al. 2026 ; Hübotter et al. 2026 ) . Applying these ideas to long-horizon coding leaves two challenges. First, training-time corrective information can encode persistent constraints and local corrections from observations relevant to one decision. Without explicit scopes, local corrections may be diluted in long contexts or propagated to unrelated decisions. Second, models without instruction-following adaptation may ignore constraints and feedback, so teacher reminders may not produce reliable distillation targets. This raises a practical question: Can we internalize scope-specific corrective information during training so that a coding agent can operate without reminders at inference time? We address this with On-Policy Reminder Distillation (OPRD) , which combines Multi-Scope Reminder supervision with outcome-based routing on rollouts from a reminder-free student. For failed trajectories, we construct reminders from task, trajectory, and execution/validation records. A Global Reminder converts final-test diagnostics into trajectory-level guidance. A Turn-local Reminder is anchored to an observation and applies only to the next decision. This combines trajectory-level feedback with local correction without propagating local feedback to unrelated decisions. For reliable supervision, our two-stage cold start uses Coding SFT to establish coding capabilities and Instruction-Following (IF) SFT to improve responsiveness to constraints, feedback, and reminders. Both models are initialized from the IF checkpoint. Based on executable verification, OPRD routes successful rollouts to autoregressive self-imitation and failed rollouts to reminder-conditioned distillation without reinforcing erroneous decisions. For failures, the teacher re-evaluates student-generated prefixes under reminders, while the student matches the teacher distribution through Top-100 reverse KL. OPRD internalizes scope-specific corrections, enabling the student to act on corresponding constraints and feedback without reminders at inference time. We evaluate OPRD on SWE-bench Verified , SWE-bench Pro , and SWE-bench Multilingual ( OpenAI 2024 ; Deng et al. 2025b ; Yang et al. 2025 ) . OPRD improves the benchmark-averaged task resolution rate by 11.3 points over the open-source Qwen3.5-4B base model. Under the same diagnostic protocol, the trained OPRD model reduces the aggregate error-event count across the four categories from 446 to 222, a 50.2% reduction relative to the base model. Our main contributions are threefold: • Multi-Scope Reminder supervision for long-horizon coding. We combine a Global Reminder that provides guidance across the trajectory with Turn-local Reminders that target only the next decision, enabling process-level supervision at complementary temporal scopes. • On-Policy Reminder Distillation with Outcome-Based Routing. We propose OPRD, which learns from verified successful rollouts through autoregressive self-imitation and from failed rollouts through reminder-conditioned reverse KL distillation. • A two-stage supervised cold start for reliable reminder distillation. Coding SFT followed by IF SFT provides a shared teacher–student initialization that improves responsiveness to constraints, execution feedback, and reminders, thereby providing a stronger starting point for subsequent reminder-conditioned distillation. 2 Related Work Figure 2: Overview of OPRD. Repository-Level Software Engineering Agents. SWE-bench established executable evaluation on repository issues ( Jimenez et al. 2024 ) . SWE-agent , AutoCodeRover , and Agentless support issue resolution through tool interfaces, program-aware search, and structured repair pipelines ( Yang et al. 2024 ; Zhang et al. 2024 ; Xia et al. 2025 ) . Training resources include agent trajectories and verifiers ( Pan et al. 2025 ; Jain et al. 2025 ) , generated or freshly collected tasks ( Yang et al. 2025 ; Badertdinov et al. 2025 ) , and reinforcement learning from executable outcomes ( Wei et al. 2025 ) . SWE-Compass and KAT-Coder further study broad capability evaluation and coding-agent training ( Xu et al. 2025 ; Zhan et al. 2025b ) . Feedback-Guided Reasoning and Reflection. ReAct conditions actions on observations, while Reflexion and self-refinement use feedback to revise subsequent attempts ( Yao et al. 2023 ; Shinn et al. 2023 ; Madaan et al. 2023 ) . FTTT formulates feedback use as test-time optimization ( Li, Lyu, and Wang 2025 ) . Tool-grounded critique, search, and code-specific execution or runtime verification further guide correction ( Gou et al. 2024 ; Zhou et al. 2024 ; Chen et al. 2024 ; Zhong, Wang, and Shang 2024 ) . FLARE extends process feedback through a generative reward model for inference and post-training ( Xu et al. 2026a ) . These approaches motivate learning to use corrective signals without requiring runtime Reminder injection. On-Policy and Privileged-Context Distillation. On-policy distillation obtains teacher supervision on student-generated sequences to reduce train–inference mismatch ( Agarwal et al. 2024 ) . MiniLLM and DistiLLM study reverse or skew KL objectives ( Gu et al. 2024 ; Ko et al. 2024 ) , with extensions using entropy-aware or black-box feedback ( Jin et al. 2026 ; Ye et al. 2025 ) . Context distillation internalizes behavior induced by auxiliary instructions, demonstrations, or scratchpads ( Snell, Klein, and Zhong 2022 ) ; privileged-context methods additionally condition teachers on verified solutions or other training-only information ( Zhao et al. 2026 ; Penaloza et al. 2026 ) . OPCD matches student trajectories to a context-conditioned teacher through reverse KL ( Ye et al. 2026 ) . OPRD combines explicitly scoped Reminders with outcome-based routing for long-horizon coding. 3 Method Long-horizon coding agents must not only generate correct code, but also consistently follow user constraints throughout multi-turn interactions and adapt their behavior to tool responses and execution feedback. However, final verification outcomes provide limited process-level supervision for constraint adherence and feedback use at different scopes. To address this limitation, we propose O n- P olicy R eminder D istillation (OPRD) , which distills the behavior of a teacher conditioned on Multi-Scope Reminders into a student that operates without reminders at inference time. (3) 3.2 Multi-Scope Reminders The constraints and feedback received by a long-horizon coding agent operate over different temporal scopes. Task-level constraints, such as permitted modification scope, interface requirements, and output format, must hold throughout the trajectory. In contrast, test errors, tool responses, and file inspection results typically pertain to a specific decision point. Nevertheless, the final verifier compresses the entire trajectory into a binary outcome and cannot represent these distinct scopes. Treating all corrective information as a single trajectory-wide prompt may cause local errors to be diluted by the long context, whereas propagating local feedback to all subsequent turns may introduce irrelevant or conflicting supervision as the interaction state evolves. We therefore organize corrective information from a failed trajectory into a Multi-Scope Reminder. A Global Reminder provides persistent task-level guidance across turns, while a Turn-local Reminder is aligned with a specific decision point and highlights environmental feedback that the model ignored or misinterpreted at that point. For a failed trajectory τ \tau , its Multi-Scope Reminder is represented as R ⁡ ( τ ) = ( r G , { ( k , r k L ) } k ∈ ℐ τ ) , ℐ τ ⊆ { 1 , … , K − 1 } , R(\tau)=\left(r^{G},\{(k,r_{k}^{L})\}_{k\in\mathcal{I}_{\tau}}\right),\qquad\mathcal{I}_{\tau}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,K-1\}, (4) where r G r^{G} denotes the Global Reminder, r k L r_{k}^{L} denotes the Turn-local Reminder anchored to environment observation o k o_{k} , and ℐ τ \mathcal{I}_{\tau} is the set of observation indices associated with local corrective signals. The Global Reminder remains visible throughout the teacher’s evaluation of assistant turns. In contrast, r k L r_{k}^{L} is visible only when the teacher evaluates the assistant turn a k + 1 a_{k+1} following o k o_{k} , and does not propagate to later turns. Instruction-Following (IF) SFT first strengthens the model’s ability to understand and respond to explicit constraints and feedback. During OPRD, Multi-Scope Reminders serve as training-time privileged information available only to the teacher, and the behavior they induce is distilled into a student that does not observe reminders. Reminder Construction. When a trajectory contains a correctable failure, we construct reminders post hoc from the task, trajectory, interaction feedback, and final test results. We determine the scope of each corrective signal and then assign its functional category. The Global Reminder contains one category, Test-Outcome Feedback , which converts final-test diagnostics into trajectory-level guidance. Turn-local Reminders target correctable decisions identified from each environment observation and assistant action. They comprise four categories: Tool-Feedback Recovery for responding to explicit tool feedback; Scope and Source Grounding for evidence-grounded localization and modification; Scenario-Condition Reasoning for task-specific execution conditions; and Post-Edit Control and Validation for controlled post-edit verification. We retain grounded and actionable candidates, discarding ambiguous, redundant, unverifiable, reference-patch-revealing, or unsupported content. Detailed category definitions, generation prompts, trigger rules, and filtering procedures are provided in Appendix C . 3.3 Two-Stage Supervised Cold Start The base model first acquires capabilities for repository understanding, tool interaction, and code editing through Coding SFT. However, coding competence alone does not ensure that the model will follow constraints and feedback that arise during an interaction. A standard coding model may still ignore user requirements, tool responses, test errors, or harness rules that are already present in the context. As the student is updated, subsequent rollouts expose different states and errors, keeping reminder supervision aligned with the distribution visited by the current policy. Outcome-Based Routing. After each rollout, an executable verifier determines whether it succeeds but does not provide fine-grained credit assignment. A successful rollout provides executable evidence that the student’s current behavior can solve the task and can therefore serve directly as an autoregressive self-imitation signal. Applying the same objective to a failed rollout would instead increase the likelihood of erroneous decisions. OPRD thus routes successful rollouts to a self-imitation branch and failed rollouts to a reminder-conditioned distillation branch. For the current rollout batch ℬ \mathcal{B} , we define ℬ + = { τ ∈ ℬ ∣ c ⁡ ( τ ) = 1 } \mathcal{B}_{+}=\{\tau\in\mathcal{B}\mid c(\tau)=1\} and ℬ − = { τ ∈ ℬ ∣ c ⁡ ( τ ) = 0 } \mathcal{B}_{-}=\{\tau\in\mathcal{B}\mid c(\tau)=0\} . Our diagnostic analysis highlights process-level failures throughout repository-level problem solving. We introduce OPRD, which uses Multi-Scope Reminders as training-time privileged information and applies outcome-based routing to on-policy rollouts. A Global Reminder converts final-test diagnostics into trajectory-level guidance, while Turn-local Reminders anchor observation-derived corrections to the next assistant decision. OPRD routes successful rollouts to autoregressive self-imitation and failed rollouts to Reminder-conditioned reverse-KL distillation. Its two-stage supervised cold start, consisting of Coding SFT followed by Instruction-Following SFT, improves responsiveness to these corrective signals. Experiments show that OPRD improves task resolution and reduces adherence failures. By internalizing trajectory-level feedback and local corrections into model parameters, OPRD enables long-horizon coding agents to operate without Reminders at inference time. Appendix C Reminder Construction Details C.1 Construction Procedure and Functional Taxonomy Reminder construction and Reminder categorization describe two distinct parts of our pipeline. Construction specifies how a candidate is obtained, filtered, inserted, and verified, whereas the functional taxonomy specifies which coding-agent failure the retained Reminder addresses. These two attributes are independent of the temporal scope used by OPRD: a Global Reminder provides persistent final-test guidance, while the runtime construction procedure below is used to construct and evaluate observation-anchored Turn-local Reminders. We construct runtime Reminder candidates from SWE-rebench V2 tasks and naturally occurring coding-agent trajectories. For each trajectory, we align the task requirement, assistant action, tool call, tool result, next assistant action, and subsequent validation outcome. A candidate is retained only when its trigger is observable without hidden correctness labels, its desired next action is local and behaviorally verifiable, and its text neither reveals a reference patch nor invents a new task requirement. Correctness is determined separately by the executable ReBench evaluator; a trajectory alone is used only as behavioral evidence. Passive and active construction modes. A passive Reminder is appended immediately after a tool result exposes a concrete failure, ambiguity, scope drift, or validation signal. It is one-shot: the next assistant decision observes it, but it does not automatically persist to later turns. An active Reminder is evaluated at a controlled pre-action boundary. We first obtain a probe action, use it only to determine whether the trigger fires, discard it when an intervention is required, insert the Reminder into the same visible state, and reroll the action. The active verifier then evaluates the rerolled tool choice, arguments, target path, edit scope, and immediate follow-up behavior. Thus passive construction mines recovery from observed feedback, whereas active construction provides a matched intervention for measuring causal behavior change. C.2 Global Reminder Construction and Evidence Judge Global Reminders are constructed only after a complete coding attempt has been rejected by executable verification. Unlike a Turn-local Reminder, which is anchored to one observation and governs the next decision, a Global Reminder summarizes the previous patch and its verifier artifacts and is placed at the input of the next full retry. A candidate is eligible only if it contains a non-empty patch, receives zero benchmark reward, is not marked non-retryable, is successfully applied in the verification environment, and is not accepted by the benchmark. These gates exclude empty or unapplied patches, successful attempts, and infrastructure-only records. The evidence judge parses the benchmark result JSON and raw test log, records available pass/pending/fail counts and touched files, deduplicates failures with a shared root cause, and selects at most five signals. Root-cause-bearing interface, argument, syntax, reference, symbol, path, and signature failures are ranked before assertion, command, and generic failures. The resulting verification_feedback states what failed and where the evidence came from, but does not reveal a reference patch or prescribe an unsupported implementation. It is visible to the teacher throughout the retry trajectory. Example. Suppose a patch applies cleanly but leaves the targets no_system_header_includes and test_wrap_static_fns failing, with three neighboring targets passing. The generated Global Reminder reports the successful patch application, the 3/2 pass–fail summary, the touched files, and the two unresolved targets, then asks the agent to re-read the task and reconstruct a consistent fix from the implicated function signatures and command flow. This evidence distinguishes an implementation failure from a malformed patch without exposing the hidden solution. We map them into four mutually exclusive reporting classes without introducing new fine-grained categories. After the most specific original category is selected for an error event, its coarse class is fixed by Table 6 . A trajectory may contain multiple independent events, but a single event receives exactly one most-specific category; multiple Reminders are allowed only when the visible evidence supports distinct risks and distinct next actions. R1: Tool-Feedback Recovery. This class covers commands, searches, edits, compilation, paths, validation conditions, and toolchain failures that can be identified deterministically from the latest tool response. R2: Scope and Source Grounding. This class combines constraints on the initial patch surface with evidence about the true production path, caller, source value, and data flow. R3: Scenario-Condition Reasoning. This class covers branch, default, fallback, cache, classification, and one-condition repairs. It separates the failing scenario from sibling scenarios that must retain their previous behavior. R4: Post-Edit Control and Validation. This class applies after a production edit. It prevents unsupported expansion of the patch and uses concrete assertions, tests, compiler output, diffs, and formatting signals to complete a local repair. Appendix D Diagnostic Error Annotation For each task, deterministic rules first identify failures exposed by explicit tool and execution signals. GPT-5.4 then receives the original problem and the complete agent trajectory to classify the remaining context-dependent failures. The combined annotations use Tool-Feedback Recovery, Scope and Source Grounding, Scenario-Condition Reasoning, and Post-Edit Control and Validation. For case-level analyses, each fine-grained category is counted at most once per trajectory. This post-hoc analysis is distinct from the online judge in Fig. 9 : the online judge operates only on the current visible prefix and decides whether to intervene, whereas diagnostic annotation audits a completed trajectory for aggregate error statistics.
